England short-term let rules: what changes in 2027

England is catching up with Scotland and Wales. A national register for short-term lets is expected in March 2027, planning changes have been promised, councils already charge second homes premiums, and mayors are getting a visitor levy power. Here is where each one stands, with the official source for every fact.

In short: what changes in England in 2027

  • Register: a mandatory national registration scheme is expected to begin in March 2027 (GOV.UK, updated 2 Oct 2026). No regulations, portal or fee yet, so you cannot register today.
  • Planning: the C5 short-term let use class is still only a proposal. Today your local planning authority decides whether letting a home is a material change of use (London: 90-night rule).
  • Second homes: since 1 Apr 2025 councils can charge up to a 100% council tax premium. 54 of the 55 English council areas we track charge one.
  • Tourist tax: the Visitor Levy Bill was introduced on 4 Nov 2026. It still has to pass Parliament, and no levy applies yet.
  • Do now: check your council, ask your council how registration will work locally (as GOV.UK advises), and get an email when any of this changes.

1. The national registration scheme Coming

The Levelling-up and Regeneration Act 2023 gives the government the power to set up a register of short-term lets. In February 2024 the government chose (DCMS statement):

  • A mandatory, national scheme covering all of England (61% of consultation responses supported this).
  • Designed to be "light touch, low cost and simple to use", delivered mainly online.
  • It would still consider a threshold so people who let their home only occasionally are not over-regulated.
  • The register will not affect hotels, hostels or B&Bs.

Status: not in force. GOV.UK now says it is expected to begin in March 2027 (updated 2 Oct 2026). Who runs it, how often you register, what information is needed and any fee have not been published.

What this means for you

You cannot register yet. Until the scheme opens, GOV.UK tells hosts to contact their council about how registration will work locally (GOV.UK).

2. Planning: the C5 use class and permitted development Proposed

In 2023 the government consulted on a new use class, C5 Short Term Let, defined as "use of a dwellinghouse that is not a sole or main residence for temporary sleeping accommodation for the purpose of holiday, leisure, recreation, business or other travel" (consultation document). Under the proposals:

  • Existing short lets meeting the definition would move into C5 automatically, without needing permission.
  • New permitted development rights would allow changes between homes (C3) and short lets (C5) without permission where short lets are not a local issue.

In February 2024 the government said it will introduce the use class, the permitted development rights and flexibility for homeowners to let their own main home for up to 90 nights a year (DCMS).

Status: proposal only. No legislation has been made.

What this means for you

  • Today your local planning authority decides whether letting a property is a material change of use (GOV.UK).
  • In Greater London the 90-night rule applies (count your nights).
  • For the detail, see do I need planning permission for a holiday let?

3. Article 4 directions

An Article 4 direction lets a council remove permitted development rights in an area, so that change needs a planning application (Planning Practice Guidance).

Under the 2023 proposals, a new permitted development right would let a home switch to a short let without an application, and areas would only see a change where a council removed that right with an Article 4 direction (consultation document). That right does not exist yet, so there is nothing for a council to remove for short lets today.

35 of 55 English council areas we track: council pages record a search that found no short-let control area or Article 4 direction aimed at holiday-let use.

In the councils we track

Where those councils do have Article 4 directions, they mostly cover things like turning houses into small HMOs (Bath and North East Somerset, Bournemouth, Christchurch and Poole, Brighton & Hove, Canterbury, Liverpool, Rother, City of York) or building alterations in conservation areas, not holiday lets. Each council page gives the search date and source.

4. Second homes council tax premiums In force

  • Since 1 Apr 2025, councils in England can charge up to a 100% premium on second homes (furnished, nobody's main home), so the bill can be up to double.
  • A council must decide at least a year before the financial year it starts.
  • Holiday lets valued for business rates do not pay it (MHCLG guidance).

What this means for you

If your home is a furnished second home that is nobody's main home and is not valued for business rates, your council can charge up to double. Find your council above. How it works and the exceptions: England second homes premium guide.

5. Overnight Visitor Levy (tourist tax) Bill introduced

On 4 Nov 2026 the government introduced the Visitor Levy Bill in the House of Commons (MHCLG). It builds on the 10 Sep 2026 announcement (MHCLG) that mayors and Foundation Strategic Authority leaders will get the power to charge an Overnight Visitor Levy:

  • It must be charged as a percentage of the accommodation cost, not a flat fee, so lower-cost stays pay less.
  • Ten mayors have said any levy in their areas would be no higher than 5%.
  • Local consultation is required before a scheme starts; Foundation Strategic Authorities need unanimous support.
  • Temporary accommodation, shelters and refuges would not be charged; leaders can add limited local exemptions (for example camping pitches).
  • Accommodation providers would account for and pay the levy to the authority.
  • The government still expects spending plans by early 2028 once powers are in use.

The Liverpool City Region Mayor says a levy could raise up to £18m a year there (LCRCA). York and North Yorkshire have said no decision will be made without a full public consultation.

What this means for you

No levy has been set anywhere in England yet: the Bill still has to pass Parliament, and each area then decides whether to use the power. See Liverpool, Sefton and the UK visitor levy map.

Common questions

When does the England short-term let register start?

GOV.UK says the mandatory national registration scheme for short-term lets in England is expected to begin in March 2027 (guidance updated 2 Oct 2026). No regulations, registration portal or fee have been published yet, so you cannot register today.

Will the register apply to hotels and B&Bs?

No. The government said in February 2024 that the register and the planning changes are focused on short-term lets and will not affect hotels, hostels or B&Bs.

Is the C5 use class law yet?

No. The government consulted on a C5 short-term let use class and permitted development rights in 2023 and said in February 2024 that it will introduce them, but no legislation has been made. Until then your local planning authority decides case by case whether letting a home is a material change of use. In Greater London the separate 90-night rule applies.

Can my council use an Article 4 direction to stop holiday lets?

An Article 4 direction removes permitted development rights in an area. Under the 2023 proposals, a new permitted development right would let homes switch to short-term lets without an application, and areas would only see a change where a council removed that right with an Article 4 direction. Because that right does not exist yet, no English council can use one for short lets today. Existing Article 4 directions in the areas we track cover things like small HMOs and building alterations, not holiday-let use.

Is there a tourist tax in England?

Not yet. On 4 Nov 2026 the government introduced the Visitor Levy Bill in the House of Commons. It would let mayors and Foundation Strategic Authority leaders charge an Overnight Visitor Levy as a percentage of the accommodation cost. Ten mayors have said any levy in their areas would be no higher than 5%. The Bill still has to pass Parliament, and no levy applies yet.
